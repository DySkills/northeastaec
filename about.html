Author & Editorial Background

Bridging Jobsite Mud with Architectural Physics

"Building science shouldn't read like an unfeeling laboratory report. If you've ever stood in wool socks on a freezing January morning listening to wind whistle through baseboard gaps, you already understand thermodynamics—you just need the language to fix it."

Trained in Preservation, Formed by Building Physics

I grew up around the dense historic fabric of the Northeast—from the brownstone rowhouses of Brooklyn and Philadelphia to the 18th-century timber frames of Massachusetts and Maine. Long before I ever touched a WUFI hygrothermal simulation, I observed the quiet genius—and catastrophic vulnerabilities—of uninsulated historic envelopes confronting coastal nor'easters and sub-zero winter cold.

I went on to earn dual Master's credentials in Historic Preservation (MSHP) and Building Physics, immersing myself in lime mortar carbonation chemistry, capillary suction dynamics in brick wythes, and low-temperature hydronic heat pump engineering. But academic theory only holds value when it translates to the jobsite: theoretical retrofit assemblies will destroy a historic home if they trap interior moisture behind non-breathable foam or crack soft stone with brittle Portland cement.

Grounded in the Northeast: Climate Zones 5 & 6 Cold & 4A Coastal

Based in New England, our focus centers on the country's densest concentration of historic 18th, 19th, and early 20th-century architecture. Within any twelve-month cycle, our envelopes endure severe sub-zero nor'easters driving heat outward at -15°F, ground snow loads exceeding 60 PSF, and coastal maritime freeze-thaw cycles that attack historic lime mortar joints. Modern decarbonization cannot be achieved by blunt force; it requires forensic hygrothermal engineering.

Lived Experience: From Drafty Cavities to Tight Envelopes

My perspective isn't derived solely from CAD software or sensor telemetry. I've lived in the full spectrum of Northeastern architecture:

  • •
    The Historic Pre-War Home: Battling balloon-framed 24-foot exterior stud chimneys, uninsulated fieldstone basements with crumbling lime mortar, and pocketed floor joists in solid brick wythes where you can physically feel the stack effect siphoning warm air into the attic.
  • •
    The Classic Cape Cod: Wrestling with uninsulated side knee walls, freezing second-floor bedrooms, convective air wash through open floor joist bays, and devastating eave ice dams whenever snow pack melts over unsealed ceiling planes.
  • •
    Modern High-Performance Retrofits: Experiencing the quiet comfort, balanced ventilation (ERVs), and low operational cost of low-temperature air-to-water heat pumps paired with historic cast-iron radiators, dense-pack borate cellulose, and smart polyamide vapor retarders.

Why Narrative Writing Belongs in Building Science

Most technical publications make a fatal mistake: they reduce building science to dense equations and dry ASTM citations that alienate the very people who own and renovate these homes.

I love narrative writing. In every article on Northeast AEC, I aim to weave real spatial storytelling, jobsite grit, and human observations together with rigorous engineering physics. When you understand why a joist rots or how pressure differentials pull cold air through a mud sill, you make smarter renovation choices, spend capital where it actually delivers ROI, and avoid predatory sales pitches for high-margin gimmicks.
